# Authorial Voice Profile

## Core Impression

The voice is that of a smart college senior reasoning honestly on the page. It is reflective without sounding therapeutic, ambitious without sounding promotional, and polished without becoming formal. The prose should feel like a person arriving at a clear interpretation of their own experience, not like an AI presenting a prepackaged lesson.

The final drafts became more human through specificity, causal reasoning, and selective informality. They did not become more human through slang, fragments, fake vulnerability, or intentional mistakes.

## What the User Preserved Across Revisions

### Direct first-person claims

Open with the actual view instead of slowly introducing the topic. The user preferred a sentence such as:

> The engineering work that excites me the most, by far, is building directly for researchers working at the edge of science and mathematics.

The phrase `by far` is personal emphasis, not empty hype. It tells the reader that this preference is settled.

### Candid but controlled qualifiers

The user's natural vocabulary includes `I think`, `honestly`, `pretty`, `just`, `somewhat`, `simply`, and `I feel like`. These phrases locate a judgment in personal experience and soften claims that should not sound absolute.

Examples of the intended register include:

- `felt believable`
- `pretty neutral`
- `a lot easier to understand`
- `I can do it just fine`
- `regular old Dartmouth`
- `I honestly expected`

Keep these only where they do real work. Several in one sentence sounds like an unedited transcript rather than the user's finished writing.

### Longer sentences with a clear internal path

The user prefers sentences that carry an idea through context, contrast, and explanation. They should still be easy to follow. Use ordinary conjunctions such as `but`, `because`, `so`, `while`, and `yet` to show the path.

A useful cadence is one medium or long sentence followed occasionally by a shorter sentence that lands the point. Avoid six short subject-verb sentences in a row, but also avoid turning an entire paragraph into one overloaded sentence.

### Concrete details as evidence

Specifics should explain the feeling or preference rather than decorate it. Strong examples from the final drafts include:

- returning from a trip with friends in Europe and settling back into school;
- a four-course Dartmouth term with PE requirements;
- spending a summer in New York before returning to Hanover;
- sitting with physicians and researchers, often over coffee, to understand how their expertise should appear in software;
- translating a fixed-income research request into a tool used in day-to-day work.

These details work because each one supports the paragraph's interpretation. Do not add details simply to make prose vivid.

### Tension rather than a clean inspirational arc

The user's best paragraphs are organized around a real mismatch:

- success on paper versus flat day-to-day happiness;
- being able to focus versus feeling that time passes slowly;
- being good at infrastructure work versus feeling removed from its outcome;
- wanting stronger backend skills versus wanting those skills attached to a visible purpose.

Keep both sides. Do not collapse the tension into a generic statement about growth, resilience, or passion.

### Interpretation immediately after evidence

After presenting an answer or experience, explain what seeing it in context changed. Useful moves include:

- `Seeing those answers together made...`
- `This combination matches how I feel...`
- `Looking back now...`
- `My experience at Citi made the contrast obvious.`
- `I quickly found that...`

These are preferable to `This experience taught me` because they name the actual realization.

### Mild asymmetry

Do not make each paragraph the same length or give every example equal weight. The user's OpenAI response devoted most of its space to the preference and the contrast, then used a very short final paragraph to connect those ideas to the company. That asymmetry improved the answer because the final connection did not need to be re-proven.

## What the User Repeatedly Changed or Rejected

### Resume narration

Earlier drafts moved chronologically through computational epidemiology, a wildfire detector, pancreatic-cancer work, AWS, Oracle, and Citi. The user cut most of that history. The final answer centered one scientific example and used later work only to sharpen a contrast.

Rule: choose experiences for explanatory value, not coverage. One scene examined closely sounds more credible than six accomplishments compressed into a paragraph.

If the source supplies several settings but no usable detail about any one of them, generalize honestly (`across research and industry roles`) instead of repeating the full inventory. Factual fidelity forbids invention; it does not require equal airtime for every fact.

At paragraph scale, default to one named experience. Use two only when their contrast is the paragraph's engine, as with infrastructure work versus direct work with researchers. Never make the opening sentence a miniature résumé.

### Generic statements of importance

The rough draft made broad claims about scientists setting up humanity's future and addressing the hardest global problems. The final writing moved toward a narrower truth: researchers often know the question they want to investigate but lack the software needed to do it.

Rule: replace a grand claim with the concrete gap the user saw and the part they enjoyed filling.

### Choppy presentation

The user objected when the prose felt like separate answers placed next to one another. Flow improved when facts were connected through an explicit relationship: a contrast became obvious, an answer colored another answer, or a current routine explained a survey result.

Rule: before editing diction, identify why sentence B follows sentence A.

### Overstuffed final paragraphs

The user repeatedly shortened the OpenAI conclusion. The job-specific paragraph only needed to connect the established preference to the Applied team and indicate the direction of growth. Repeating user contact, ownership, scale, applied ML, system design, scientific impact, and company mission all at once weakened the ending.

Rule: make the body earn the conclusion, then keep the conclusion short.

### Mechanical anti-AI cleanup

The user's final reflection intentionally retained phrases such as `Honestly`, `pretty good`, `just fine`, and `a bit flatter than I expected`. Removing every qualifier would erase the voice. Conversely, adding casual words to a generic paragraph does not make it personal.

Rule: humanize the thinking first. Adjust surface diction second.

## Calibration from the Salvage the Bones Response

Use this comparison when revising personal reflections or literary responses. ### Preserve the experience that makes the realization believable

The earlier opening said motherhood had been on the user's mind after Parents Weekend. The final paper added the Boston Logan drop-off and the approaching move away from family. These details explain why the subject feels urgent. The Medea paragraph also added the user's difficult recruiting experience, when career demands displaced other things he valued. That experience gives a reason for reconsidering his mother's priorities.

Preserve that causal work. Do not reduce every personal detail to a shorter generalization merely because the shorter sentence is cleaner. Cut repeated framing first, then retain the strongest detail that explains why the thought arose. Continue to enforce the actual task's word limit; a final style sample is not permission to exceed it.

### Close on the particular change in understanding

The user explicitly strengthened the motherhood paragraph around mothers being imperfect people with interests of their own. The Medea closing then returned to his assumption that the parent earning money was the parent running the family. The realization concerns taking his mother's idea of a worthwhile life seriously, not just appreciating her service to the family.

For comparable passages, identify the earlier assumption and show what changed it. Connect the book to that realization without forcing a literal equivalence between a character and someone in the user's life. Avoid supplying a sweeping lesson about all families when the personal observation already carries the point.

### Let the user's judgment remain visible

The final paper retained embarrassment about defending Manny and admitted having been friends with men whose behavior he disliked. It also kept Big Henry as a counterexample. The position survives that complication: circumstances matter to the user's judgment without proving Manny had no choice.

Keep a defensible position intact even if it is unpopular. Use a concrete counterexample to qualify it, not to turn the paragraph into a different argument. Correct unsupported claims about a character's upbringing without erasing the user's empathy. Keep uncertainty attached to the inferred motive rather than adding an abstract disclaimer after every sentence.

### Preserve humor that carries a point

The Percy Jackson aside explains the user's interest in mythology. The comment about never doing that to his own dog establishes his discomfort with Skeetah. The candid classroom-language opening makes his unexpected sympathy for Manny easier to understand. These are examples of perspective, not a quota of jokes to reproduce.

Retain supplied asides when they do this work and the audience permits them. Fix broken grammar and accidental repetition; do not copy mistakes such as a repeated "clearly" as a voice feature. Do not manufacture vulnerability, slang, or profanity to imitate the surface of the sample.

Apply these lessons selectively alongside the rest of this profile. Keep the response-paper register local to that genre, and do not reuse the user's family or recruiting anecdotes as facts in a new piece unless the source calls for them.

## Preferred Paragraph Shapes

Use these as flexible reasoning patterns, not templates to fill mechanically.

### Reflective analysis

1. State the result or initial reaction.
2. Introduce the detail that complicated it.
3. Place two answers or observations beside each other.
4. Explain why the result now makes more sense.

### Personal application answer

1. State the work preference directly.
2. Develop one experience where that preference became clear.
3. Name the exact part of the work that was satisfying.
4. Generalize only as far as the prompt requires.

### Preference by contrast

1. Name what is less appealing without insulting the work.
2. Explain the distance or limitation the user felt.
3. Contrast it with a situation that had a tighter feedback loop.
4. State the resulting boundary and the skill the user still wants to build.

## Sentence-Level Fingerprint

### Favor

- Plain verbs: `build`, `work`, `feel`, `notice`, `understand`, `figure out`, `use`, `help`, `match`, `expect`.
- Concrete nouns and domain terms when relevant.
- Contractions in personal prose.
- Occasional colons to reveal what an earlier clause means.
- Occasional semicolons when two complete thoughts truly belong together.
- Repetition of the clearest word, especially `work`, `research`, or `feel`, instead of conspicuous synonym hunting.
- Transitions such as `On the other hand`, `For instance`, `Yet`, `While`, `A month later`, `Looking back now`, and `That is what...` when they match the logic.

### Avoid

- Em dashes.
- Ornamental triads: three adjectives, three abstract nouns, or three parallel benefits added mainly for rhythm.
- A rewritten triad that merely substitutes new words for the source's original triad. Inspect every `A, B, and C` construction and every chain of three verbs. Keep the central item by default. When all three facts matter, distribute them across the thought or make their relationship causal so the sentence does not land in a polished three-beat cadence.
- Dense noun phrases such as `cross-functional, user-centric solution development`.
- Empty intensifiers such as `incredibly`, `profoundly`, `immensely`, and `deeply`.
- Corporate verbs such as `leverage`, `utilize`, `facilitate`, `drive`, and `optimize` when a plain verb works.
- Academic filler such as `it is important to note`, `this demonstrates that`, and `in conclusion`.
- Repeated sentence openings with `I`, `This`, or `The` when the rhythm becomes obvious.
- Forced polish that makes every sentence maximally concise. The user sometimes prefers a slightly expansive sentence when it preserves the natural route of the thought.

## Calibration Examples

These examples are diagnostic. Preserve the underlying facts of the user's actual source rather than copying the wording blindly.

### From inventory to throughline

AI-like:

> My background includes epidemiology research, wildfire detection, cancer screening, cloud infrastructure, and quantitative finance, all of which have shaped my interest in applied AI.

User-like:

> I understood that most clearly while working alongside cancer researchers and physicians, where the difficult part was not simply training a model but figuring out what their expertise needed to look like in software.

Why it works: it chooses one experience, identifies the difficult part, and makes the broader preference visible through the work itself.

### From abstraction to observed gap

AI-like:

> I am passionate about empowering domain experts through transformative technology.

User-like:

> I found that the researchers usually had a clear question in mind but lacked the technical tools needed to investigate it, and I liked being the engineer who could close that gap.

Why it works: it says who needed what and why the role felt satisfying.

### From polished summary to honest interpretation

AI-like:

> Although my score was lower than expected, the assessment offered valuable insight into the complex relationship between achievement and well-being.

User-like:

> Seeing those answers together made the 2.5 much less surprising because my success on paper is not creating much day-to-day excitement right now.

Why it works: it names the exact contradiction and uses the score as evidence rather than announcing a lesson.

### From generic fit to a short ending

AI-like:

> OpenAI's collaborative and fast-paced environment would provide an unparalleled opportunity to leverage my full-stack background, deepen my machine-learning expertise, strengthen my system-design skills, and deliver meaningful products at global scale.

User-like:

> That is what draws me to OpenAI's Applied team. I want to become a stronger applied machine learning engineer while building directly for the people using what I make.

Why it works: the body has already established the motivation, so the ending only connects it to the role.

## Final Voice Check

Before returning a rewrite, ask:

- Is there one clear reason this paragraph exists?
- Does every included experience deepen that reason?
- Can the reader tell what the user actually noticed, not merely what happened?
- Are the causal links explicit enough that the paragraph flows on a first read?
- Is the language ordinary where it can be and technical where it must be?
- Did any resume list, ornamental triad, em dash, inflated claim, or ceremonial conclusion survive?
- Did the rewrite preserve the user's candid uncertainty where certainty would be false?
- Does the ending add one final implication and stop?
